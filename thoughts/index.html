When worry starts spinning, your body gets ready for danger and your mind keeps searching for a way out. That isn't a lack of faith. It's a body and mind God made to protect you, working overtime. The steps below help both slow down, and none of them ask you to pretend you're fine.

Do them in order, or pick just one. A hard day is not a failed day.

The steps

  1. One long breath out

    Breathe in through your nose. At the top, take one more short sip of air. Then let it all out slowly through your mouth, longer than the breath in. Do this three to five times.

    Ready when you are.

    Why it helps: In a month-long randomized trial from Stanford, five minutes a day of this “cyclic sighing” lifted mood more than mindfulness meditation did, and slowed people's breathing (Balban et al., Cell Reports Medicine, 2023). If it makes you dizzy, stop and breathe normally.

    Want a longer breathing time? Try Breath Prayer.

  2. Name the feeling

    Pick one word, or type your own, and say it quietly to God: “Lord, I feel…”

    “Search me, God, and know my heart. Try me, and know my thoughts.”Psalm 139:23 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: In a brain-imaging study, putting a feeling into words calmed the amygdala, the brain's alarm center, more than other ways of looking at the same upsetting images (Lieberman et al., Psychological Science, 2007).

  3. Step back from the loudest thought

    Write the thought that keeps coming back. We'll put six words in front of it. The thought doesn't have to go away. You're just noticing it's a thought, not the final word.

    “bringing every thought into captivity to the obedience of Christ,”from 2 Corinthians 10:5 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: Psychologists call this “defusion.” In one experiment, adding “I am having the thought that…” in front of negative statements about oneself made them less upsetting to read (Healy et al., The Psychological Record, 2008). It's a small study, and it didn't make the thoughts feel less true. It made them easier to sit with.

  4. Speak to your soul

    The psalm writer didn't only listen to his feelings. He spoke to them. Try it with your own name, the way you'd talk to a friend.

    “Why are you in despair, my soul? Why are you disturbed within me? Hope in God! For I shall still praise him for the saving help of his presence.”Psalm 42:5 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: In a series of experiments, people who talked to themselves by name or as “you,” instead of “I,” felt less distress and saw a stressful task as more of a challenge than a threat (Kross et al., Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2014).

  5. Give the worry an appointment

    If a worry keeps circling, choose a time later today, for no more than 30 minutes, to think it through and pray about it. When it shows up before then, tell it: “Not now. Later.”

    “Therefore don’t be anxious for tomorrow, for tomorrow will be anxious for itself. Each day’s own evil is sufficient.”Matthew 6:34 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: In a randomized study of 53 people who worry a lot, two weeks of a daily 30-minute worry period, at a set time and place, reduced worry, anxiety and insomnia more than the comparison approach (McGowan & Behar, Behavior Modification, 2013). The researchers called the results preliminary.

  6. Write it down, then hand it over

    Write down everything that's spinning, with no grammar and no order. Then read it once as a prayer and give it to Him.

    This stays only in your browser on this device. We never see it.

    “casting all your worries on him, because he cares for you.”1 Peter 5:7 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: In a study of people with chronic worry, writing expressively about their worries reduced a brain signal that is enlarged in anxiety, and the researchers suggest writing may “offload” worries from working memory (Schroder, Moran & Moser, Psychophysiology, 2018).

  7. Listen, look, and rest

    Turn on the stream or rain sound above, or step outside or to a window. For one minute, just listen and look at what He made.

    “He makes me lie down in green pastures. He leads me beside still waters.”Psalm 23:2 (WEBU)

    Why it helps: After a stressful task, people's stress response (measured by skin conductance) tended to settle faster while they listened to nature sounds than to traffic and other noise (Alvarsson et al., International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, 2010). A review of 36 studies found natural sounds were linked to lower stress and better mood (Buxton et al., PNAS, 2021). And people who took a 90-minute walk in nature reported less rumination than people who walked in a city (Bratman et al., PNAS, 2015). If you can, take a walk with Held.

The research behind this page

Every study below is real and linked so you can read it yourself. Most are small, and none of them tested this page as a whole. These are tools for a hard moment, not a treatment.

  1. Balban MY et al. Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal. Cell Reports Medicine, 2023. Read it (PMC9873947)
  2. Lieberman MD et al. Putting feelings into words: Affect labeling disrupts amygdala activity in response to affective stimuli. Psychological Science, 2007. Read it (PDF)
  3. Healy HA et al. An experimental test of a cognitive defusion exercise: Coping with negative and positive self-statements. The Psychological Record, 2008. doi:10.1007/BF03395641
  4. Kross E et al. Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: How you do it matters. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 2014. Read it (PDF)
  5. McGowan SK & Behar E. A preliminary investigation of stimulus control training for worry: Effects on anxiety and insomnia. Behavior Modification, 2013. Abstract (ERIC)
  6. Schroder HS, Moran TP & Moser JS. The effect of expressive writing on the error-related negativity among individuals with chronic worry. Psychophysiology, 2018. doi:10.1111/psyp.12990
  7. Alvarsson JJ, Wiens S & Nilsson ME. Stress recovery during exposure to nature sound and environmental noise. Int J Environ Res Public Health, 2010. Read it (PMC2872309)
  8. Buxton RT et al. A synthesis of health benefits of natural sounds and their distribution in national parks. PNAS, 2021. doi:10.1073/pnas.2013097118
  9. Bratman GN et al. Nature experience reduces rumination and subgenual prefrontal cortex activation. PNAS, 2015. doi:10.1073/pnas.1510459112

What we looked at and left out

  • The “5-4-3-2-1” senses exercise. It's widely recommended, but we couldn't find a study that tested it on its own, so we haven't presented it as proven. Noticing what's around you is part of the last step.
  • Binaural beats and “healing frequencies.” A 2023 systematic review found the research on binaural beats inconsistent, with more studies against the main claim than for it (Ingendoh et al., PLOS ONE, 2023). We use real water recordings instead.
